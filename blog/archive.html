What Will Your Executive Jet Cost in the First Year?

Build a 12-month budget for each finalist before making an offer. Use your expected flight hours and home airport, then ask the team that would operate the aircraft to estimate crew, training, hangar, insurance, fuel and maintenance costs using those same assumptions.

Separate recurring costs from work you expect to pay for soon after delivery, such as an upcoming inspection or cabin repairs. Confirm what any advertised maintenance program covers and ask about transfer requirements and charges.

Compare the finalists with a base budget and a higher-cost scenario that includes an unscheduled maintenance event. That gives you a clearer view of how much cash to set aside after closing and whether each aircraft fits your first-year budget.

Commercial aircraft purchases

Compare the Engines Behind the Airframe Price

Ask for a separate records package for each installed engine. Match the serial numbers against the offer and have your technical team review shop visit history, current condition and the remaining life recorded for limited-life parts.

Use that review to build a base case and an early-shop-visit case for each candidate. Ask the team to estimate how long the aircraft or engine could be out of service and how the operation would cover that gap. Compare the aircraft offers using the same assumptions about future flying.

Executive jet purchases

Set the Scope of a Pre-Purchase Inspection Early

Ask a maintenance facility familiar with the model to review the aircraft’s records and propose an inspection scope before you commit to a delivery date. Give the shop time to look for gaps in the logs and identify prior repairs or upcoming maintenance that merit a closer look.

Set a written process for what happens when the shop finds a discrepancy. Identify who reviews the findings, how the parties will decide what gets addressed before delivery, and when the buyer can make a decision. A clear process gives both sides a way to handle surprises without improvising under deadline.

Commercial aircraft purchases

How Well Does a Used Aircraft Fit Your Fleet?

Compare a prospective aircraft with the fleet it would join. Have engineering, flight operations and cabin teams review the engine variant, cockpit equipment, seat layout and galley configuration against your operating needs.

Build a delivery-to-service plan before comparing offers. List the cabin work, spares and training you expect to need, who would arrange each item and when the aircraft could start flying the schedule. That gives your team a common basis for comparing aircraft with different configurations and delivery dates.

Executive jet purchases

Choose a Jet for the Trips You Actually Fly

Start your aircraft search with a list of the trips you took in the past year. Record the airports, passenger count, baggage and how often you flew each route. Then identify the handful of trips that would put the most pressure on the aircraft you buy.

Ask each candidate’s flight operations team to work through the same scenarios, including a full cabin, a hot-weather departure and a winter trip with baggage. Seeing the assumptions side by side helps you decide which capabilities are worth paying for and where an occasional fuel stop is acceptable.

Aircraft Availability and Leasing: What Buyers Should Watch Heading Into 2027

Airlines and aircraft investors are entering the final months of 2026 with a familiar challenge: securing the right aircraft at the right time. Airbus reported 547 commercial aircraft deliveries in the first nine months of 2026, up from 507 a year earlier, but its order backlog stood at 9,310 aircraft at the end of September.

The leasing market also remains active. In October, aircraft lessor Avolon announced agreements to order 250 Boeing and Airbus aircraft, subject to shareholder approval, reflecting continued demand for modern, fuel-efficient fleets. Meanwhile, industry discussions at ISTAT EMEA suggest some narrowbody supply conditions may gradually normalize, even as widebody availability and engine constraints remain important concerns.

For aircraft buyers, availability is only one consideration. Delivery timing, maintenance status, engine support, lease terms, and the aircraft’s suitability for the intended operation can materially affect a transaction. For sellers and leasing partners, well-organized technical records and a clear understanding of buyer requirements can help facilitate productive discussions.

Rock Limited’s perspective: as airlines and operators plan for 2027, disciplined aircraft sourcing and early engagement with qualified counterparties remain valuable. Market conditions vary by aircraft type, age, configuration, and jurisdiction; no particular price or delivery outcome is guaranteed.

Sources: Airbus nine-month 2026 results (October 28); Reuters on Avolon orders (October 9); ISTAT market analysis (October 5).
